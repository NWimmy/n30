2026 NFL preseason composite ratings

How this model works

A composite team rating built from real prior-season play-by-play, an Elo-based season and playoff simulator, and a devigged sportsbook comparison — every input and every constant validated against real historical outcomes before being trusted, not hand-picked.

Composite rating

What goes into each team's number

Every team gets a composite rating built from a weighted blend of six real inputs: offensive and defensive EPA (expected points added) per play, split by pass and rush, net special-teams EPA, and quarterback quality. The four EPA splits are opponent-adjusted — a two-way regression that solves for each team's real offensive and defensive strength simultaneously from every play of the season, so a team that happened to face a slate of bad defenses doesn't get an inflated number with zero correction — and blended across the two most recent seasons (more recent weighted higher) rather than judged on one season's noisy sample alone.

Weights are fit with ridge regression against real next-season win totals, then checked with leave-one-season-out cross-validation: refit on every season but one, scored on the held-out season, repeated for every season in the window. A fit that only looks good in-sample and falls apart out-of-sample gets rejected rather than trusted. Ridge specifically (instead of an ordinary regression) because two of the six inputs — passing offense and quarterback quality — measure a genuinely overlapping thing; ridge splits credit between them properly instead of arbitrarily favoring one.

Quarterback quality

Real per-play production, not a preseason guess

QB quality is each team's presumptive starter's real EPA per dropback from last season (the passer with the most real dropbacks, unless a known real 2026 offseason change — a trade, signing, or retirement — is manually corrected). A starter with a small real sample — a backup, or a rookie who barely played — gets that number blended toward a below-average default in proportion to how few dropbacks they actually threw, rather than trusted at face value on a handful of snaps. An unresolved QB competition blends every named candidate's own real prior production into one number rather than guessing a winner.

What we tested and didn't adopt

Every real experiment, kept honest either way

Net special-teams EPA (kicking, punting, coverage, and returns combined into one number) was added as the sixth input after a real, held-out accuracy gain over the five-input model — but unlike the four team EPA splits, opponent-adjusting or multi-year-blending special teams itself did NOT help once tested; it stays a single-season, un-adjusted number. Special-teams units simply turn over too much year to year — a new kicker, punter, or coordinator — for older seasons to add anything but noise.

Player-level running back and receiver production, plus offensive-line pass-protection proxies (sacks and QB hits allowed), were all tested as additional inputs and found redundant with the team-level EPA splits already in the model — they mostly re-measure signal the model already has, rather than add new information.

A roster-durability input (real weekly injury-report designations) showed a real improvement on its own, but once combined with the other six inputs it produced an unstable, counterintuitive weight — the fitted model would have slightly penalized healthier teams, backwards from that input's own honest real-world relationship to wins. Read as a fitting artifact on a modest sample rather than a real effect, and left out.

Blending quarterback quality across multiple seasons — the same treatment that helped the four team EPA splits — was tested and didn't help either. A starting quarterback's real situation (scheme, supporting cast, health, even which team) changes enough year to year that older seasons hurt more than they smooth out noise; QB quality stays a single-season number.

A market-based preseason win-total input was considered — the sportsbook market is a well-documented strong predictor precisely because it prices in real-world information (coaching changes, injuries, roster judgment) this model can't see — but was ruled out on data grounds: no free, reliable historical archive of preseason win-total lines exists to validate it against real outcomes the way every other input here was.

We also checked a genuine question of interest: does "defense wins championships" hold up? Refitting the same six weights against real historical PLAYOFF wins instead of regular-season win total found the opposite — offense, especially quarterback play, matters MORE for championship success, not less. A real, interesting finding, but a pure diagnostic: the live model stays fit against regular-season wins, which is what its own win/loss and playoff-odds projections are actually built to predict.

Season simulation

How preseason ratings turn into playoff odds

The composite rating anchors each team's starting Elo. Every scheduled game for the season is simulated 10,000 times: each simulated result updates both teams' Elo (scaled by real margin of victory, not just win/loss), which then feeds into the next game on that team's schedule. The real 2026 playoff bracket is simulated too — division winners seed 1-4, wild cards seed 5-7, the field reseeds through the Conference Championship, matching the real NFL format. Known simplification, not hidden: seeding tiebreaks use win total plus random jitter rather than the real NFL tiebreaker cascade (head-to-head, division record, strength of victory, etc.).

Market comparison

What "Mkt" columns mean, and their real limits

Market odds are hand-compiled, point-in-time sportsbook lines, devigged to strip out the book's built-in margin before comparing against this model's own fair odds — each team's playoff line devigged against its own two-way make/miss pair, the Super Bowl board normalized as a whole so all 32 teams' odds sum to 100%. These are snapshots, not a live feed — a line quoted here can move well before you'd see it reflected on this page again, and "--" means no market line was found, not a 0% model probability.

Honest caveats

What this model is and isn't

This is a preseason research model for one person's own use, published for interest, not a professional betting product. It has no injury-availability adjustment yet (beyond the QB-quality confidence blend above), and playoff seeding tiebreaks are a disclosed simplification, not the real NFL cascade. It is not betting advice, carries no guarantee of predictive accuracy, and a favorable "edge" against a market line is not a guaranteed win rate. Every input and constant above was tested against real historical outcomes before being trusted — and just as honestly, several candidates that looked promising were tested and left out once the real numbers didn't back them up.
